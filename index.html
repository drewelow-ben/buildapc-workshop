III. Hardware Compatibility

Quick Guide — The Big Picture

Short, easy checks to make sure parts will work together.

  • CPU & board: Make sure the CPU fits the board like matching puzzle pieces. You must have an exact matching socket.

  • Memory (RAM): Make sure the RAM type (DDR3 / DDR4 / DDR5) matches the board and there is space for it.

  • Power: Make sure the power supply has enough power and the right plugs for your parts.

  • Size: Make sure big parts like the graphics card and cooler fit inside the case.

  • Drives: Make sure the board has places for your hard drives or SSDs.

  • Cooling: Make sure there is room for fans and air to move around the parts.
What to do if you're not sure

IV. Resourcing Equipment: New vs. Used

When you plan to build your own PC, there is a choice to use new hardware or used/refurbished components (or a combination). Both choices can work. This section explains more.


Quick answer

  • New parts — may be easier to use, may come with a warranty, and have the newest features.
  • Used parts — much cheaper, far more eco-friendly, and often have enough features to last for years into the future.

Why people pick new parts

  • They usually work right away.
  • Most new parts include a manufacturer warranty.
  • You get current features like faster speeds and newer ports.
  • Less time needed for testing and troubleshooting.
  • Easier to ensure compatibility

Why people pick used parts

  • Used parts are cheaper, so you can build systems on a much smaller budget.
  • Reusing parts helps the environment by keeping electronics out of landfills.
  • Local reuse groups and volunteers may donate parts.
  • Users get more experience with troubleshooting and repair skills.

Pros and cons at a glance

New parts

  • Pros: Reliable, warranty, up-to-date specs.
  • Cons: More expensive, higher manufacturing impact on the environment.

Used parts

  • Pros: Lower cost, eco-friendly, great for hands-on learning.
  • Cons: Often sold "as-is", may fail, may need cleaning or simple repairs.

Simple testing checklist for used parts

  1. Clean off dust and look for visible damage (cracks, burn marks, missing pins).
  2. Test the part in a working system.
  3. Check that power supplies are the right wattage and in good shape.
  4. Boot to BIOS or a basic OS to confirm the system starts.
  5. Run a memory test or quick disk check when possible.
  6. Donate broken or failed components to a local electronics recycling center.
  7. If a part smells like burned electronics, do not use it.

Where to find used parts and help

Look for donations or low-cost parts from local reuse centers, repair cafes, makerspaces, and community groups. You can also check online marketplaces.

V. Safety

Key safety considerations regarding tools, electricity, and component handling.


Before you start

  • Work on a stable, non-conductive surface in a well-lit area; avoid carpeted floors.
  • Disconnect power: unplug the power cord and switch off the power supply before working inside a case.
  • Remove jewelry and avoid loose clothing; keep long hair tied back.

Electrostatic discharge (ESD)

  • Ground yourself before touching components: use an anti-static wrist strap or touch the metal case frequently.
  • Handle circuit boards and memory by the edges; avoid touching pins, gold contacts, and chips.
  • Keep components in anti-static bags or on an anti-static mat when not installed.

Tools and handling

  • Use the correct, non-magnetic or low-magnetism tools and the right size screwdriver to avoid slipping and damaging components.
  • Keep a parts tray or labeled containers for screws and small items.
  • Do not force connectors or components; check alignment and retention mechanisms before applying pressure.

Power supply & batteries

  • Never open a power supply—its capacitors can hold dangerous charge. If you suspect a faulty PSU, replace it or have it tested by a professional.
  • Disconnect the power cord and flip the PSU switch off before working inside the case.
  • Remove the CMOS battery carefully if required and recycle batteries at appropriate facilities.

Cooling and thermal paste

  • Apply thermal paste sparingly and evenly; clean old paste with isopropyl alcohol before reapplying.
  • Ensure CPU coolers and case fans are mounted securely and that airflow is not obstructed.

Sharp edges and heavy lifting

  • Case edges can be sharp—use gloves if needed and take care when reaching inside.
  • Take care when lifting a heavy case. Get help if you're uncertain about lifting with ease.

Liquid and fire safety

  • Keep liquids away from electronics. If a spill occurs, unplug the system immediately and let it dry completely before powering on.
  • Have an appropriate (electrical) fire extinguisher available in workshop spaces.

Testing and powering up

  • Double-check connections before powering on. Start with minimal components (CPU, one stick of RAM, GPU if required) when troubleshooting.
  • Listen and smell for abnormal noises or burning—power off immediately if you detect either.

If something goes wrong

  • Stop and ask for help if unsure. Do not attempt risky repairs beyond your experience.
  • Document serial numbers and take photos during disassembly to help reassembly and warranty claims.
